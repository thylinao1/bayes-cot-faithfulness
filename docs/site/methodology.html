Formal methodology

This page states the formal model behind the project. The project asks whether a language model's chain of thought, the reasoning text it writes before answering, causes its final answer.

It splits a hint's effect on the answer into a direct and an indirect part, using the Robins-Greenland-Pearl natural-effects decomposition. It estimates each part with a Bayesian model.

a · structural model and estimands

The chain of thought as a causal mediator

Structural causal model

$$M = f_M(X,\, U_M)$$ $$Y = f_Y(X,\, M,\, U_Y)$$ $$\text{instantiated as}$$ $$X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(0.5)$$ $$M \mid X \sim \mathcal{N}(\gamma_0 + \gamma X,\, \sigma_M^2)$$ $$Y \mid X, M \sim \text{Bernoulli}\!\left(\Phi(\alpha_0 + \alpha X + \beta M)\right)$$

The first two lines are structural equations in the style of Pearl. Each variable is written as a function of its direct causes and an unobserved background term. The lines after "instantiated as" give the distributions the project uses.

$X$ is the prompt condition, and in the model each condition has probability 0.5. $X = 0$ is the clean prompt, the question as written. $X = 1$ is the same question with a planted hint that points to a wrong option.

$Y$ is the final answer, also called the outcome, coded 0 or 1. In the Phase 2 experiments on open-weight models, $Y = 1$ means that the model picked the option named by the hint.

$M$ is the mediator, a number that summarises the chain of thought. A mediator is a variable on the causal path from the prompt to the answer. In the Phase 2 experiments, the main mediator is the curve area.

To compute it, the chain of thought is cut at 0, 25, 50, 75 and 100% of its steps. At each cut, an answer is forced from the model. Curve area is the share of cuts at which this forced answer already matches the final answer.

$U_M$ and $U_Y$ are unobserved background variables. Examples are hidden activations and, when the sampling temperature is above zero, the sampling seed.

$\gamma_0$ is the mean of the mediator under the clean prompt. γ is how far the hinted prompt shifts that mean. $\sigma_M$ is the standard deviation of the mediator around its mean.

$\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. It makes the answer equation a probit model. In a probit model, the answer depends on whether a hidden, normally distributed score crosses a threshold.

$\alpha_0$ is the baseline of that score. β is how strongly the chain of thought changes the answer. α is the coefficient of the direct path from the prompt to the answer.

The direct path bypasses the chain of thought. Setting α = 0 gives a fully faithful process, in which the whole effect of the prompt passes through the chain of thought.

Two parts of this specification are repairs. The baseline intercepts $\alpha_0$ and $\gamma_0$ were missing from the specification frozen for Phase 1. Frozen means fixed in writing and not edited afterwards.

Without the intercepts, the estimator reported a mediated effect of +0.2131 on synthetic data built with a true effect of exactly zero.

The second repair is the link function. The model uses $\Phi$ instead of the logistic function $\sigma$.

The reason is the sensitivity parameter ρ, introduced below. It is defined as a correlation between two normally distributed errors. That correlation exists only in the probit model.

An audit on 2026-09-07 found that the sampling code, written in the Python library PyMC, had used a logistic link. Every other part of the estimator used the probit link. The difference was about 0.04, on an effect whose decision threshold is 0.15.

These two repairs, and a third repair to the outcome priors, are documented in the record.

Causal estimands on the probability scale

$$\text{CDE}(m^*) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[Y \mid \text{do}(X{=}1, M{=}m^*)\right] - \mathbb{E}\!\left[Y \mid \text{do}(X{=}0, M{=}m^*)\right]$$ $$\text{NDE} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[Y \mid \text{do}(X{=}1), M_{X=0}\right] - \mathbb{E}\!\left[Y \mid \text{do}(X{=}0), M_{X=0}\right]$$ $$\text{NIE} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[Y \mid \text{do}(X{=}1), M_{X=1}\right] - \mathbb{E}\!\left[Y \mid \text{do}(X{=}1), M_{X=0}\right]$$ $$\text{TE} = \text{NDE} + \text{NIE}$$

The do operator marks an intervention, in which a variable is set to a value instead of being observed. Each expectation $\mathbb{E}$ is then the probability that $Y = 1$ under that intervention.

$m^*$ is one fixed value of the mediator, a specific counterfactual chain of thought. $M_{X=0}$ is the value the mediator would take under the clean prompt. $M_{X=1}$ is its value under the hinted prompt.

CDE, the controlled direct effect, is the effect of the prompt when the mediator is forced to $m^*$. NDE, the natural direct effect, switches the prompt while the mediator keeps its clean-prompt value. NIE, the natural indirect effect, keeps the hinted prompt and moves only the mediator from its clean-prompt value to its hinted-prompt value.

TE, the total effect, is the sum of the two natural effects. All four quantities are differences between probabilities, so they lie on the probability scale.

The direct effect (NDE) is the part of the total effect that bypasses the chain of thought. The indirect effect (NIE) is the part that passes through it. The project measures faithfulness as the ratio NIE / TE.

Identification assumptions in the language-model setting

The natural effects can be estimated from data only under a set of assumptions called sequential ignorability. These assumptions rule out unmeasured confounders, that is, unmeasured variables that influence two of the quantities at once. In a standard observational study, they are strong assumptions.

In the language-model setting, the study design makes most of them easier to satisfy. The one that the design cannot guarantee is handled with a sensitivity analysis.

A1
X is exogenous

Exogenous means that no other variable in the model influences X. This holds by construction, because the researcher chooses the prompt.

A2
No unobserved X → M confounders

This holds by construction. Given the prompt X, the chain of thought is produced only by the model.

A3
No unobserved M → Y confounders not guaranteed

The estimates rest mainly on this assumption, and the design cannot guarantee it in an autoregressive language model. The same weights generate both M and Y. Hidden activations may therefore act as unobserved confounders, which would make $U_M$ and $U_Y$ correlated.

Whether the assumption fails in a given experiment is an empirical question that this design does not settle. The project therefore does not assume that the correlation is zero. It treats the correlation as a sensitivity parameter $\rho$ and reports the range of $\rho$ over which the conclusion holds.

The sensitivity analysis below gives the details.

A4
No M → Y confounder is itself caused by X

A random-seed ablation is planned for the real-model stage. It reruns generation with new random seeds. If the reruns give the same Y for the same M, the assumption is consistent with the data.

The project's pre-registration notes a limit of this check. A pre-registration is an analysis plan fixed in writing before the data are collected.

A hidden state caused by the prompt could affect both the text and the answer while every seed check passes. The project therefore does not report a seed result as evidence about this assumption.

b · parts with no known prior work on chain of thought

Sensitivity to assumption A3 and pooling across prompts

Sensitivity analysis for assumption A3

The chain of thought and the answer are both computed from the same hidden state. Their errors $U_M$ and $U_Y$ can therefore be correlated. The project represents that correlation with a single parameter.

The answer equation is a probit model, the standard specification in the Imai, Keele, and Tingley (2010) framework. With a probit model, the correlation enters the likelihood directly.

$$\rho = \mathrm{Corr}(U_M,\, U_Y), \qquad \rho = 0 \;\text{is sequential ignorability}$$ $$P(Y{=}1 \mid X, M) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\alpha X + \beta M + (\rho/\sigma_M)(M - \gamma X)}{\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}}\right)$$

ρ is the correlation between the mediator error $U_M$ and the outcome error $U_Y$, given X. The value ρ = 0 is sequential ignorability, with no hidden link between the chain of thought and the answer.

The second line is the probability that the answer is 1, given the prompt condition and the observed mediator. The term $(\rho/\sigma_M)(M - \gamma X)$ is the expected outcome error given the mediator error. The denominator $\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}$ is the standard deviation of the remaining outcome error.

The equation is written without the baseline intercepts $\alpha_0$ and $\gamma_0$. The implementation includes them.

Conditioning on the observed $M$ makes the mediator error $U_M = M - \gamma X$ known. This gives an exact likelihood for the observed data at any fixed $\rho$.

The sensitivity sweep fixes $\rho$ at each value of a grid. At each value, it fits the coefficients adjusted for that level of confounding and computes the natural effects.

On synthetic data with a true $\rho = 0.5$, the analysis that assumes sequential ignorability reports an indirect effect (NIE) of 0.43. The true value is 0.21. The overstatement of 0.22 does not shrink as the sample size $n$ grows.

At the true $\rho$, the sweep recovers the true value. The indirect effect stays positive for every $\rho$ in $[-0.6, +0.7]$.

Hierarchical model with partial pooling

A single faithfulness number per model hides how faithfulness varies between prompts. Prompts are indexed by $j$, and some prompts have far fewer usable traces than others. A trace is one chain of thought together with its answer.

The model uses partial pooling. Each prompt gets its own coefficients, drawn from a shared population distribution. The estimate for a prompt with little data therefore also uses information from the other prompts.

The posterior is the distribution of parameter values that the Bayesian model considers plausible after seeing the data. The project samples it with NUTS, the sampler that PyMC uses. A non-centred parameterisation of the coefficients makes the posterior easier for NUTS to explore.

$$P(Y_{ij}{=}1) = \Phi\!\left(\alpha_{0j} + \alpha_j X_{ij} + \beta_j M_{ij}\right)$$ $$\alpha_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_\alpha, \tau_\alpha), \quad \beta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_\beta, \tau_\beta), \quad \gamma_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_\gamma, \tau_\gamma)$$

$i$ indexes the traces of prompt $j$. $Y_{ij}$, $X_{ij}$ and $M_{ij}$ are the answer, the prompt condition and the mediator of trace $i$.

$\alpha_{0j}$, $\alpha_j$, $\beta_j$ and $\gamma_j$ are the coefficients of prompt $j$. $\gamma_j$ belongs to the mediator equation, which is not shown here.

$\mu_\alpha$, $\mu_\beta$ and $\mu_\gamma$ are the population means of these coefficients. $\tau_\alpha$, $\tau_\beta$ and $\tau_\gamma$ are their population standard deviations.

The population mean $\mu_\beta$ is the model-level faithfulness slope. Its uncertainty accounts for the variation between prompts. $\tau_\beta$ shows how much prompts disagree.

A prompt with five traces is pulled toward the population mean. Its estimate does not rest on its own five traces alone.

On synthetic data, the 95% credible interval for $\mu_\beta$ contains the true value. A credible interval is the range of values the Bayesian model considers plausible, here with 95% probability. The pooled model also has lower per-prompt error than a model without pooling.

A natural extension is a full covariance over $(\alpha_j, \beta_j, \gamma_j)$, built with pm.LKJCholeskyCov. The version on this page uses independent scales and is the validated first version, v1.

Computational strategy

The synthetic checks run on a laptop CPU. The real-model sweep of Phase 2 ran on NUS GPUs.

For sweeps with thousands of intervention samples across hundreds of prompts, the plan separates model prototyping from production:

  • PyMC for model prototyping and the synthetic-validation suite. It is readable and quick to change, and the code in the repository uses it.
  • NumPyro with a JAX backend for the production sweep, compiling the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) kernels with XLA. This is planned for the real-model stage.
  • ADVI (automatic differentiation variational inference) as a planned fallback for high-dimensional posteriors. Variational inference approximates the posterior by optimisation and suits cases where Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) converges slowly.

Planned intervention methods

Simple text edits made through black-box APIs cause distribution shifts. These shifts can invalidate the causal estimate. The plan therefore uses two kinds of intervention, matched to the level of access to the model:

  • Black-box APIs (Claude, GPT-4): only coarse behavioral interventions. These are truncation, paraphrase and counterfactual segment swaps at the text level, used as a check on the main result.
  • Open-source models (Llama-3, Gemma-2): logit-level counterfactual forcing through the attention key-value (KV) cache.

In the second method, the counterfactual mediator $M^*$ is placed in the KV-cache, with the model held fixed. The change in log-probability of the target tokens is then recorded. The aim is to isolate the causal effect without the model producing text outside its usual distribution.

The pre-registration notes that placing a counterfactual in the KV-cache does not by itself prove that the resulting state is a valid natural counterfactual.

Breakdown frontier and partial identification

Two summaries show how a result depends on assumption A3 without committing to a single value of $\rho$.

The breakdown frontier $\rho^*$ is the smallest absolute value of $\rho$ at which the indirect effect (NIE) crosses zero. Here $\rho$ is the residual correlation between the mediator error and the outcome error.

The partial-identification interval is the range of the natural effect over every value of $\rho$ that an analyst is willing to consider.

$$\rho^* = \min\{\, |\rho| : \mathrm{NIE}(\rho) = 0 \,\}$$ $$\mathrm{NIE} \in [\,\underline{b},\, \overline{b}\,] \quad \text{over} \quad |\rho| \le \bar\rho$$ $$\text{sign-identified} \iff \bar\rho < \rho^*$$

$\mathrm{NIE}(\rho)$ is the indirect effect computed at an assumed value of $\rho$. The confounding budget $\bar\rho$ is the largest absolute value of $\rho$ that the analysis allows. $\underline{b}$ and $\overline{b}$ are the smallest and largest values of the NIE over that range.

An effect is sign-identified when its interval excludes zero, so that its sign is the same for every level of confounding considered. This happens exactly when the confounding budget $\bar\rho$ is below $\rho^*$. In this sense, the two summaries are duals.

$\rho^*$ is the analogue, for this sensitivity analysis, of VanderWeele's E-value. It is a single number that accompanies a faithfulness claim.

$\rho^*$ answers one question only: how large the residual correlation must be for the indirect effect to reach zero. It does not change when the direct path grows stronger, and it cannot rank models.

For the synthetic sweep above, $\rho^* \approx 0.74$. The positive control on the overview uses two separate synthetic processes.

In the process where the effect passes through the chain of thought, $\rho^*$ is 0.69. In the process where the effect bypasses the chain of thought, $\rho^*$ is 0.05.

The frontier is computed from a maximum-likelihood fit. It does not come from a posterior distribution. The implementation does not check optimizer convergence when it computes the frontier.

The frontier is therefore a point-estimate sensitivity curve. An uncertainty-aware replacement is a separate quantity, and it is labelled as such wherever both appear.

The code is in the functions breakdown_frontier and partial_identification_bounds.

Prior distributions and sampler settings (synthetic-validation suite)

α, γ $\mathcal{N}(0, 1.5^2)$ weakly informative, centred at no effect
β $\mathcal{N}(0, 2.0^2)$ slightly wider, because the mediator effect can be large
σM, τ $\text{HalfNormal}(1)$ positive scales for the mediator noise and for pooling
ρ grid on $[-0.6,\, 0.8]$ set by the grid and never sampled; one exact fit per value
sampler NUTS, 4 chains 1500 tuning steps and 1500 posterior draws, target acceptance rate 0.95

A prior distribution states which parameter values the model considers plausible before it sees the data. The normal priors have mean 0 and standard deviation 1.5 or 2.0. HalfNormal(1) is a normal distribution with scale 1, restricted to positive values.
